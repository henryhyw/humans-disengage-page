Humans disengage,
reasoning models persist.

Thinking LRMs spend more tokens on the items they get wrong. Humans spend less time on the items they get wrong. On the very same problems.

Han-yu Wang · The University of Hong Kong · ORCID

01  ·  The puzzle

A surface similarity. What lies beneath?

Thinking large reasoning models (LRMs) take longer on harder problems. So do humans. de Varda and colleagues (2025) recently reported this cross-item alignment in matched human–LRM data and read it as evidence of shared deliberation.

But cross-item alignment is one thing. What happens on a single item — when a particular agent attempts a particular problem? Does the agent that registers an item as hard then spend the right amount of thought on it?

The same item-level data conceals an opposite within-item pattern: humans and LRMs do not allocate deliberation the same way.

02  ·  The diagnostic

Two levels: registration and allocation.

The paper separates two behavioral questions that previous work conflates:

  • Registration — does deliberation length track which items are hard? Measured as a cross-item Spearman ρ between an agent's deliberation length and the human-RT proxy for difficulty.
  • Allocation — given an item, where does the agent spend more thought? Measured as a within-agent, outcome-conditioned, item-controlled contrast: Cohen's d on log deliberation, wrong − right.

Registration aligns with task difficulty. Allocation reveals what control policy the agent is running under. Cross-item alignment alone cannot tell them apart.

Two diagnostics. Same data. They come apart on H-ARC.

03  ·  The headline finding

A sign reversal, holding items fixed.

LRM allocation (well-powered)
Cohen's d = +1.47 to +3.13
All 4 well-powered thinking LRMs spend longer on wrong trials than on right ones
Human allocation (item-fixed)
Slope = −0.45 log units
Same items, opposite direction: less time on the trials they get wrong
Interaction (item fixed effects)
βLRM = −0.66
95% CI [−0.81, −0.50], p = 4×10−17

Holding the item exactly fixed, humans and LRMs do not move in the same direction. Cross-item alignment masked an opposite within-item allocation policy.

06  ·  What might be producing each side

Two convergent behavioural probes.

Mechanism interpretations of behavioural signatures, not direct evidence of internal architecture. Left: on H-ARC humans, controlling for the per-trial grid-action count cuts the within-item correctness slope nearly in half — consistent with engagement-vs-abandonment. Right: length-controlled LRM trace-content features (hedging, repetition, type–token ratio) sit reliably on wrong trials — consistent with length-on-uncertainty.

6b · LRM trace-content asymmetries

The behavioural signatures suggest different control policies: human time is partly gated by engagement, LRM length tracks uncertainty-like trace features.

08  ·  Robustness — Cortes bootstrap uncertainty

Small n, but the LRM bars don't touch zero.

Cortes per-LRM wrong-trial counts are 5–8 per model. Bootstrap 95% CIs (10,000 resamples) on the within-agent d are wide — but every thinking LRM CI excludes zero. The non-thinking V3 baseline does too.

09  ·  What's next

Truncation is the discriminating test.

Cross-item alignment between LRM trace length and human reaction time, the basis of recent claims of shared deliberation, conflates two mechanisms that pull apart at the within-item level. A controlled truncation manipulation — running LRMs at fixed thinking budgets and recovering accuracy under each budget — would discriminate a length-on-uncertainty policy from a useful-search interpretation.

truncation simulator · toy predictions drag the slider to set thinking-budget fraction f
f = 1.00 · accuracy under length-on-uncertainty: — · useful-search: —

Under length-on-uncertainty, the extra tokens were rumination — accuracy holds up under truncation. Under useful-search, each token bought information — accuracy degrades with f. The curves diverge most between f ≈ 0.5 and f ≈ 0.8. Real LRM data falling on either curve discriminates the two policies.

A single "deliberation" construct conflates two policies. Item-controlled, outcome-conditioned contrasts are how you tell them apart.
